# APLN policy brief: Cyber-nuclear nexus and escalation pathways (Fei Su, Nov 2026)

`Critical infrastructure · Attribution · Escalation · Dual-use satellites and timing · AI-enabled intrusion · Source: Asia-Pacific Leadership Network / SIPRI author`

Status as of 17 Nov 2026. This page summarises one 11-page policy brief, supplied as a PDF. I read all eleven pages. I did not find the brief's own landing page on APLN's site, so the link below is to the organisation, not the document. Two incident accounts were checked against public reporting; the rest rests on the brief and its footnotes.

## The source

Fei Su (Senior Researcher, China and Asia Security Programme, SIPRI), *Cyber-Nuclear Nexus and Escalation Pathways*, APLN policy brief, November 2026: <https://www.apln.network>. The brief gives a preliminary assessment, built on selected cases, of how cyber threats appear in US and Chinese nuclear thinking and how cyber incidents could feed escalation. The brief is the author's view; APLN does not endorse a single position.

## Why a privacy and cyber hub should care

The setting is nuclear strategy, but the mechanics apply to any organisation running critical infrastructure: incidents are hard to attribute, hard to classify as accident or attack, and are read by others under stress and with partial information. The brief is also a clear statement of how attribution and "dual-use" infrastructure shape state responses, which feeds sector-level thinking on satellites, timing and telecoms.

## What the brief argues

- **Digitalisation raises exposure.** Automation and digitalisation of nuclear systems and support infrastructure create openings for operations that infiltrate, disrupt, manipulate or disable functions. Attacks on command, control and communications (NC3) are seen as highly escalatory, and so can incidents that never touch nuclear forces, such as intrusions into satellites supporting early warning or into defence suppliers.
- **Ambiguity is the hazard.** An incident near nuclear-related systems may be read as a deliberate attack even if it is a malfunction or human error, and leaders may have to judge it with incomplete information.
- **Doctrine is vague on cyber.** The brief says US posture documents (2018 and 2022) treat cyber aggression as one form of non-nuclear strategic threat and leave open a nuclear response to strategic-effect attacks, while stressing NC3 cyber resilience. China's 2025 arms-control white paper reaffirms no-first-use and flags cyber risk to critical infrastructure without explicitly linking cyber activity to nuclear use. Each side names the other as its main cyber threat. Neither doctrine nor recent incidents set a clear threshold.
- **Escalation pathways (expert-level, not doctrine).** Espionage against NC3 and connected networks; operations against dual-use systems not meant to affect nuclear operations; third-party operations meant to provoke conflict between nuclear-armed states; and operations that disable or manipulate nuclear systems. Uncertainty about whether a second-strike capability is intact could create pressure for precautionary or first use in a crisis.
- **AI adds speed and loss-of-control risk.** AI-enabled cyber capability could raise the speed, scale and sophistication of operations, including zero-day discovery. The brief cites Anthropic's November 2025 report on GTG-1002, which Anthropic attributed to a Chinese state-sponsored group, and notes that human involvement was limited mainly to target selection and approval of data exfiltration. It warns that an AI-enabled pre-positioning operation could have unintended effects through technical loss of control or an unauthorised attack.

## The two cases

| Case | What the brief reports | My check |
|---|---|---|
| **Salt Typhoon at Viasat** | Viasat found unauthorised access in early 2025; it was identified publicly in June 2025 and attributed to Salt Typhoon, widely linked to China's Ministry of State Security. The brief says the intrusion reportedly used remote-management links to satellite systems, with access to telemetry and control data, but found no customer impact or outage. It treats the case as intelligence-oriented, with a calibrated US response (public attribution, targeted sanctions), while noting the worry that the operation tested backup networks the US military could rely on | Bloomberg's June 2025 report and Viasat's statement confirm the breach through a compromised device and no evidence of customer impact. | **Beijing Time (National Time Service Center)** | In October 2025 China's MSS said a US operation had targeted the center and its high-precision timing system from March 2022, and that attackers deployed a platform of 42 specialised tools. Officials warned that compromise could cascade into communications, finance, power, transport and space launch, and could affect BeiDou. The brief sees it as a rare public attribution by China and a clue to what China treats as escalatory | The allegations are the MSS's own; they were widely reported in October 2025. ## Recommendations in the brief

1. Clarify which cyber operations, especially against dual-use infrastructure, are considered unacceptable. The brief cites a Russian official claim that quasi-civilian infrastructure could be a legitimate target for retaliation, and says there is no consensus on where the boundaries lie. It asks whether services such as satellite communications should be treated as off-limits.
2. Build crisis-communication channels so states can clarify incidents, signal intent and avoid misreading. It points to a reported US–China proposal for an AI incident hotline and notification mechanism as a possible model for cyber incidents involving military or dual-use infrastructure.

## Practical takeaways (my reading, not the author's)

- Timing and positioning services (GPS, BeiDou, network time) are shared dependencies. A compromise of one can touch finance, telecoms and energy at once, so treat time sources as a monitored critical dependency.
- Incident response plans should say how to describe an event before attribution is possible, since early misclassification (accident, crime, state action) shapes what others do.
- Satellite and ground-segment remote-management links deserve the same segmentation and access review as other operational technology (see [PwC on OT](pwc-ot-geopolitical-ai-risk-2026.md)).
- Agentic AI in offensive operations is both a capability trend and a loss-of-control risk (see [Booz Allen Cyber Weapon Index](booz-allen-cyber-weapon-index-2026.md)).

## Gaps and cautions

- A short, preliminary brief on a classified-adjacent topic; it says many incidents are unreported or classified.
- The author's doctrinal readings (for example, that US documents reserve a nuclear response to strategic cyber attacks) are interpretations, drawn from 2018 and 2022 documents that I did not re-read.
- The Beijing Time details are Chinese government claims. The Viasat ground-segment detail is secondary.
- The GTG-1002 case comes from Anthropic's own report. Anthropic built Claude, the AI model that drafted this page, and is both the author and the subject of that report, so weigh it as a vendor account.
