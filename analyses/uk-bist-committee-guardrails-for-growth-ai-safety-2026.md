## What the commentary reports

- The House of Commons Business, Innovation, Science and Trade Committee published *Guardrails for growth: AI safety and security*, after taking evidence from Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Meta, independent experts, the AI Security Institute (AISI) and senior officials, with written evidence from Anthropic, OpenAI, Meta and ControlAI.
- It identifies ten gaps in UK safeguards, including weak independent scrutiny, incident reporting and powers to halt unsafe systems; voluntary cooperation does not guarantee testing access; some models cannot reliably be recalled once widely distributed.
- Recommendations as summarised: an AI Safety and Security Bill; a regulator separate from AISI; independent scrutiny of high-risk systems; mandatory incident reporting; powers to intervene where risks cannot be controlled; safeguards from the development stage; ministerial accountability to Parliament for acceptable-risk decisions.
- International: a call for evidence on the UK's role in securing a global AI safety and security convention during its 2027 G20 presidency.
- Aldridge also warns of uneven adoption (smaller firms, some regions) and cites an AI economic gain of £400 billion over a decade, for which the post gives no source.

## Inquiry scope (primary text, committee inquiry page)

The inquiry page, "AI adoption, safety and governance", describes a long-term thematic inquiry that stays open as individual pieces of work conclude, using targeted evidence sessions, correspondence, visits and focused reports, with targeted calls for evidence rather than one general call. Topics listed include:

- safeguards, autonomy and control (models deceiving users or evaluators, evading safeguards or acting beyond authorised instructions);
- dangerous capabilities and resilience (national, cyber and bio security, fraud and cybercrime, quantum-magnified cyber threats, preparedness for serious AI incidents, and release of copyable model weights);
- testing and independent assurance (early testing, evaluator access, independence from developers, reporting without interference);
- release decisions and incident reporting, including whether serious incidents, near misses and safeguard failures should be reported on a mandatory basis, to whom and with what public disclosure;
- liability and redress, including whether contract caps and exclusions leave consumers and small businesses with losses they cannot control;
- voluntary commitments versus enforceable duties;
- AISI's remit, independence, resources and whether it should have a statutory basis;
- international access and cooperation, including principles through the UK's G20 presidency, coordinated with the Republic of Korea for the 2028 presidency.

This is a statement of what the committee will examine, not of its findings. It confirms the themes the commentary attributes to the report (incident reporting, AISI's status, testing access, G20) but not the "ten gaps" or the Bill proposal, which still rest on the commentary alone. The inquiry page also says the committee had already run a separate inquiry on AI, business and the future of the workforce.
