The article says it was originally published by Centrum Balticum.

## Core argument

AI regulation has become a tool in a contest between EU "technological sovereignty" and US "technological leadership". The article treats the two sides as overlapping in jurisdiction but unequal in control: EU law can govern how AI is placed on and used in the European market, while US public law can change whether the underlying capability is available at all.

## Part 1: competing jurisdictional claims

| Theme | What the article reports |
|---|---|
| EU reach | The AI Act applies to third-country providers, deployers, importers and distributors that place AI systems or general-purpose models on the EU market, put them into service, or whose output is intended for use in the EU |
| US reach | US export-control and national-security rules can affect access to models, compute or controlled technology. In the article's words, EU law may govern market placement or use, while US law governs supply |
| Rights-led v innovation-led | The AI Act combines harmonisation and innovation with protection of fundamental rights under the EU Charter. US federal policy is described as the opposite emphasis: Executive Order 14179 (23 Jan 2025) directs removal of measures impeding US AI leadership, and White House legislative recommendations (20 Mar 2026) ask Congress to remove barriers and pre-empt burdensome State AI laws |
| Enforcement example (EU) | 21 Aug 2026: the Dutch Data Protection Authority announced fines of €824.99 million against Uber B.V. and Uber Technologies Inc. for solely automated decisions deactivating driver accounts, in breach of GDPR Article 22 |
| Enforcement example (US) | 24 Apr 2026: the Department of Justice moved to intervene in xAI's challenge to Colorado's algorithmic-discrimination law, which the article says prompted Colorado to rewrite its regime away from the EU AI Act's ex-ante rights model |

## Part 2: EU sovereignty regulation and its limits

**The EU response.** On 3 June 2026 the Commission presented the European Technological Sovereignty Package, including proposals for a Cloud and Data Act (CADA) and Chips Act 2.0. The article highlights that CADA's explanatory memorandum says the AI Act "does not cover aspects of sovereignty". CADA proposes a four-level cloud sovereignty framework. Under it, certain third-country-controlled providers could be audited against level 3 only if the Commission recognises the country by implementing act, against four criteria: governmental access under Article 32 of the Data Act, service continuity, access to advanced technology, and procurement reciprocity. The article says this applies directly to US providers subject to US law, and that together with Chips Act 2.0 it shifts EU policy from regulating AI systems towards reducing dependence on the infrastructure beneath them.

**The US counterweight.** The article points to Executive Order 14320 (23 Jul 2025), which set up the American AI Exports Program to promote full-stack US-origin AI packages worldwide. It also cites Anthropic's statement of 12 June 2026 that a US export-control directive required it to deny foreign nationals access to Claude Fable 5 and Claude Mythos 5, causing a global suspension until the controls were lifted on 30 June 2026. The article uses this as its example of US public law altering the availability of AI capability for EU users.

**Conclusion.** The open question is whether the EU's sovereignty framework can deliver meaningful European AI sovereignty while critical AI capability stays subject to US law.

## Why it matters for privacy and cyber teams

- **Two layers of compliance risk.** The AI Act and GDPR govern lawful use in the EU, but supply continuity depends on US export-control decisions. Resilience planning for AI-dependent services should cover regulatory loss of access, not only outages or vendor failure.
- **CADA is a cloud and data-access question as much as an AI one.** The Article 32 Data Act criterion (governmental access to non-personal data) links sovereignty to the same foreign-government-access concerns that drive transfer impact assessments and DPIAs for US cloud providers.
- **Concentration and exit planning.** Reliance on a small number of US model and cloud providers is the risk the package targets. Contracts, exit and portability clauses, and fallback models become part of third-party risk management.
- **The Article 22 fine shows enforcement is already hitting automated decision-making** in the platform economy, independent of AI Act timelines.

## Gaps and cautions

- The article is a short commentary with no footnotes; the proposals, executive orders, the DPA decision and the Anthropic statement are named but not linked.
- CADA and Chips Act 2.0 are proposals. The article gives no legislative timetable, and the level 3 recognition criteria could change.
- The article does not say how a recognition decision under CADA would interact with existing adequacy or transfer mechanisms.
- The Uber fine figure is unusually large and should be confirmed against the Dutch DPA's own announcement.
- The reading that Colorado rewrote its law because of the DOJ move is the author's characterisation of causation.
