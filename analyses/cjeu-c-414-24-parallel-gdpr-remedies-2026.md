## Facts

A physician asked the operator of a physician search and review platform to delete personal data about her, and the operator refused. She sued in civil court (eh.at says in Nov 2017) for deletion and an injunction. After the GDPR applied she made a second deletion request, also refused, and complained to the Austrian Data Protection Authority (DSB) under Art. 77(1), relying mainly on the right to erasure in Art. 17 (eh.at says July 2018). The DSB rejected the complaint because it concerned the same subject matter as the pending civil suit and, in its view, parallel proceedings were incompatible with the GDPR's remedy structure. The Federal Administrative Court accepted that parallel remedies were possible in principle but dismissed the complaint as filed late. Both the physician and the DSB appealed to the VwGH.

## Questions referred

1. Can a supervisory authority dismiss a complaint because a judicial remedy under Art. 79 on the same matter has already been brought?
2. Does it matter if a first-instance merits decision has been given in the court case but is not yet final?

## The ruling (as summarised)

- A supervisory authority cannot reject an Art. 77(1) complaint solely because the data subject has already brought court proceedings under Art. 79(1). McCann FitzGerald says the Court answered both questions in the negative, so a non-final merits judgment does not change this.
- The Court relied on the text: both provisions apply without prejudice to other administrative or judicial remedies, and neither the authority nor the court has exclusive or priority jurisdiction. McCann FitzGerald says it relied largely on its earlier judgment of 12 Jan 2023 in C-132/21 (Nemzeti Adatvédelmi és Információszabadság Hatóság), which held that the Art. 77 and 79 remedies are concurrent and independent.
- Member States keep procedural autonomy but it is limited by effectiveness (rights must not be practically impossible or unduly burdensome) and equivalence (EU-law remedies must not be treated less favourably than comparable national ones).
- Dismissal would create a real risk to protection: the court case could end on formal grounds or be withdrawn, and the complaint deadline could expire meanwhile.
- **Suspension is allowed.** National law may let an authority suspend its proceedings while a court case is pending, to avoid conflicting decisions, until the court case is finally resolved, after which the authority must take the final decision into account. Matheson says Member States may address the risk of inconsistency through procedural rules, including suspension.
- The Legal 500 article does not discuss Art. 78 (judicial remedy against an authority).

## Practical points (the authors' view as summarised)

- **Controllers and processors:** expect parallel administrative and civil proceedings; a pending lawsuit does not automatically block a complaint; keep factual submissions consistent across courts and authorities.
- **Supervisory authorities:** cannot dismiss for parallel court proceedings alone; may suspend and must consider the final outcome.
- **Data subjects:** do not have to choose between a complaint and a lawsuit.

## Why a privacy and cyber hub should care

- **Litigation exposure.** One incident can trigger a complaint, a civil claim for damages or injunction, and an authority investigation at the same time, so breach-response and disclosure positions must be consistent across all of them.
- **Authority practice.** Authorities that dismissed complaints in the face of parallel claims, as Austria's did, must change course or use suspension. That affects how long complaints stay open.
- **Time limits.** The risk of a lapsing deadline was central to the Court's reasoning, which matters to anyone advising on limitation periods.
