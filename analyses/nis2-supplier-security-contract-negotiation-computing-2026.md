## Summary

The opinion piece argues that supply-chain security under NIS2 is mostly a matter of what is negotiated and agreed in the commercial contract, not of technical controls alone. Its framing is that a buyer cannot tell a supplier how to run its business, but can set the conditions under which that supplier accesses the buyer's systems and data.

## Key points (from the article's own summary box and opening)

1. **Responsibility sits with the buyer.** The piece reads Article 21(2)(d) of NIS2 as making the buyer responsible for supply-chain security risk, including breaches that start at a supplier.
2. **Put incident terms in the contract before access.** Notification triggers, timelines and audit rights should be agreed before the supplier gets access.
3. **Questionnaires record claims; audit rights verify them.** A signed security questionnaire shows what a vendor says. Audit rights written into the agreement let the buyer check it.
4. **Access should be bounded.** Supplier access should be time-limited, logged and attributable, and contract end dates should match access end dates.

## Why it matters

- It turns a legal duty into a procurement checklist: clauses on incident notification, audit and access lifecycle are the contract evidence a regulator or auditor can ask for.
- The point about end dates is easy to miss in practice. Contracts that expire while accounts and credentials stay live are a common gap.

## Practical clause checklist

This list is the hub's reading of the article's points, not the author's.

| Clause | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Incident notification trigger and deadline | Lets the buyer meet its own reporting deadlines |
| Audit and evidence rights | Turns self-declared controls into verified ones |
| Access scope, time limit, logging and attribution | Keeps supplier access bounded and traceable |
| End-of-contract access revocation | Ties contract end to access end |
| Flow-down to sub-suppliers | Extends obligations along the chain |
