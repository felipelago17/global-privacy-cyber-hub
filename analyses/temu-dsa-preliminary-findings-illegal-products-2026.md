# Temu: Commission preliminary findings on illegal-product risk mitigation under the DSA (17 Nov 2026)

`Digital Services Act · VLOP risk mitigation (Art. 35) · Online marketplaces · Product safety · Enforcement · Source: European Commission, with press reporting`

Status as of 17 Nov 2026. This page summarises the Commission's news item of 17 November 2026 and press coverage of it. The Commission's full press release (IP/26/2463) and the underlying preliminary-findings document were not reachable from my tools, so the detail below comes mainly from Euronews, with the Commission page as the headline source. These are preliminary findings, not a decision.

## The source

European Commission, *Commission preliminarily finds Temu in breach of the Digital Services Act for failure to tackle sale of illegal products*, 17 Nov 2026: <https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-preliminarily-finds-temu-breach-digital-services-act-failure-tackle-sale-illegal>. Press detail: Jonathan Benton, "EU preliminarily finds Temu broke rules on illegal and unsafe products", Euronews, 17 Nov 2026.

## What the Commission preliminarily found

The Commission's preliminary view is that Temu has not put in place reasonable, proportionate and effective measures to mitigate the risk of illegal products being sold to EU consumers. Press reports place this under Article 35 of the DSA (mitigation of systemic risks). The Commission cites three weaknesses:

1. **Product-safety controls.** Per Euronews, sellers must supply test reports for only a small share of products, and Temu often does not check the reports it receives. Checks on higher-risk goods such as electronics and cosmetics were not prioritised, and spot-check results were not followed up.
2. **Content moderation.** Moderation appears to focus on listing information rather than actual product safety. Safety is not prioritised when the comparison system surfaces products already identified as non-compliant, so safer products may be harder to find. Human moderator numbers have not grown with the product range.
3. **Sanctions.** The sanction system is described as fragmented and ineffective. Penalties fall on stores rather than the traders behind them, so a banned trader can keep selling through another store, and Temu is slow to act on repeat offenders.

## How this sits with the earlier case

- **Proceedings** opened in October 2024. **Preliminary findings** on the Article 34 risk assessment followed in July 2025.
- **28 May 2026:** the Commission fined Temu €200 million for failing to assess systemic risks from illegal products (Articles 34 and 35 in reporting; the Commission's own statement and MLex describe it as the second non-compliance decision under the DSA). Temu was required to submit an action plan under Article 75; Tech Policy Press gave an August deadline. Temu was reported as appealing.
- **October 2026:** Euronews reports the Commission approved the action plan.
- **17 Nov 2026 (this item):** a separate preliminary finding, on mitigation measures rather than risk assessment. The Commission now says Temu is still not doing enough.

## What happens next

Temu can review the findings, reply and prepare its defence. If it does not satisfy the Commission, a non-compliance decision could carry a fine of up to 6% of global annual turnover. Temu says it "respectfully" disagrees, that it invests significantly in compliance, and that it is confident of an amicable resolution (as reported by Euronews). Timing of any decision is not stated in the sources I could read.

## Why a privacy and cyber hub should care

- It shows the Commission moving from "did you assess the risk" (Article 34) to "do your controls work in practice" (Article 35). Documented controls without verification, follow-up and resourcing were treated as insufficient.
- The three findings map to familiar assurance themes: evidence checks on third-party attestations, prioritisation by risk, and enforcement against repeat actors rather than accounts. Identity-linking of sellers across stores is the same control problem as repeat-offender handling in fraud and abuse programmes.
- A second action on the same platform within six months shows how a fine, a remediation plan and a fresh investigation can run in parallel.
