## Summary

A plain-language guide to **Federal Decree-Law No. 34 of 2021 on Countering Rumours and Cybercrimes** (in force 2 January 2022; penalties tightened by Federal Law No. 5 of 2024), written for people who do not think of a WhatsApp message or an Instagram comment as a potential crime scene. Its central message is that the statute reaches further than most residents, visitors and employers assume — into private messages, into conduct by tourists and transit passengers, and into content posted from abroad — and that **online defamation and insult** (Art. 43) is the category that catches the most people because it turns on public exposure, not malice or falsity.

For this hub the note is useful as the **criminal-law counterpart to the UAE PDPL**: the article's own section on how the two statutes differ is the cleanest short statement of the boundary.

## Key points

### Scope
- Replaced the 2012 cybercrime law and extended it to deepfakes, cryptocurrency-related fraud, automated bots and deliberate misinformation during public-health crises.
- Functions as the UAE's primary criminal statute for anything done through a network, website or information system.
- **Personal reach:** everyone physically present in the UAE — citizens, residents, tourists, transit passengers.
- **Extraterritorial reach:** conduct wholly outside the UAE if the content targets the country, harms its interests, is accessible from within it, or affects a UAE citizen or resident. VPNs and anonymous accounts do not change the analysis; authorities routinely obtain account attribution from platforms.
- Attempts carry up to half the penalty of the completed offence; sharing a link to illegal content can amount to participation.

### Offence categories (practical shortlist)
Hacking and unauthorised access; electronic fraud; privacy violations; impersonation; spreading rumours or false information; online defamation and insult; content offences; attacks on government and critical-infrastructure systems.

### Online defamation and insult — Art. 43
- Using an information network or system to insult someone, or to attribute to them something exposing them to public contempt or legal consequence.
- Courts apply something close to a reasonable-person test. **Truth, sincere belief and good intentions are not automatic defences.**
- A comment or reply in a thread is treated like a published article; a **private WhatsApp message** can found a complaint.
- Typical fines **AED 250,000–500,000**, with imprisonment possible.

### Privacy violations
Photographing or recording a person without consent, or publishing their location, is an offence **whether or not** the material is ever shared.

### Penalties
Wide range: tens of thousands of dirhams for lesser offences; seven years' imprisonment or more and fines in the millions for national-security and critical-infrastructure offences; imprisonment and fine can be cumulative; **deportation** with a re-entry ban is a realistic consequence for expatriates. The article cautions that publicly reported figures vary by provision.

### Corporate and managerial liability — Art. 58
Where a cybercrime is committed through a website or online entity, **the entity and the individual responsible for managing it** (owner, partner, director) face equal liability and the same penalties. A company cannot treat an employee's misconduct through company channels as a purely personal matter.

### Settlement and leniency
Amicable settlement with the victim is available for a defined set of lower-tier provisions at any point before judgment becomes final; Art. 61 gives discretionary leniency to an offender who helps detect another crime or offender.

### Cybercrime Law vs Personal Data Protection Law
| | Federal Decree-Law 34/2021 (Cybercrime) | Federal Decree-Law 45/2021 (PDPL) |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Criminal | Regulatory, largely civil |
| Targets | Malicious acts — hacking, fraud, harassment, illegal content, defamation | How organisations collect, process and secure personal data; data-subject rights |
| Can be breached without the other | A careless data-handling failure is a PDPL matter with no cybercrime | A defamatory post is a cybercrime with no PDPL breach |

### Case note — Abu Dhabi Court of Cassation, Criminal Appeal No. 1829/2025 (29 Dec 2025)
Electronic defamation under Art. 43 read with Art. 11 of the Criminal Procedure Law. The defendant argued inadmissibility because the complaint had been pursued by the victim's lawyer. The Court held the decisive point was that the **victim personally filed the complaint with the Public Prosecution within the statutory time limit**; a lawyer following up the investigation did not invalidate proceedings, and a complaint by the victim, their legal representative or a duly authorised attorney suffices for the Public Prosecution to proceed.

## Practitioner implications

| Area | Takeaway |
|---|---|
| Corporate social media | Art. 58 makes the entity and its managing individual co-liable for posts through company accounts — approval workflows and named responsibility for corporate handles are a legal control, not just brand hygiene |
| Employee communications | Internal chat and WhatsApp groups are within scope; HR and whistleblowing policies should say so |
| Incident response | Where an incident involves insult, impersonation or leaked images, the victim's **personal, time-limited complaint** is the procedural trigger (Cassation 1829/2025) — advise early |
| Marketing and reviews | Negative statements about competitors or individuals can be prosecuted on the basis of public exposure alone; truth is not a safe harbour |
| Workforce mobility | Visitors, secondees and transit passengers are fully in scope; deportation risk for expatriate staff should feature in conduct briefings |
| Privacy programme | Keep PDPL compliance and cybercrime exposure as separate risk registers; they are answered by different controls and different advisers |
