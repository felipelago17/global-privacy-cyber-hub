## What the AMA is asking for

- **Laws protecting physicians from liability for AI-related harm.** AMA President Willie Underwood III says AI can support clinical work but patients should know who is responsible for their care.
- **Liability insurance** requirements for technology companies.
- **Disclosure** by developers of product limitations and performance data.
- **No contractual release.** Provider contracts, patient disclosures and consent forms should not be used to release developers from responsibility.
- **Right to opt out.** Per the AMA's 29 Oct framework, doctors should be able to opt out of AI tools without penalty where the tool's clinical validation, usefulness or place in the standard of care remains uncertain. Some tools should need clearer evidence of safety or efficacy.

## The framework behind it (AMA sources, excerpts)

- The AMA's Center for Digital Health and AI announced a framework on 29–30 Oct 2026 aligning liability with each party's authority and ability to prevent or mitigate harm, across developers and manufacturers, healthcare organisations and other deployers, physicians and patients. It says existing liability frameworks were not designed for this and that physicians may face liability for risks they cannot reasonably prevent while patients may struggle to get remedies. The AMA's principle: physicians answer for clinical judgment they can meaningfully exercise, and developers for the systems they build and the risks within their control.
- The AMA's Oct 2026 issue brief lists concerns, including physicians being held responsible for errors they cannot reasonably detect, relying on systems they were not told were in use or could not decline, and indemnification clauses shifting financial responsibility.
- Policy adopted by the AMA House of Delegates is dated 10 Nov 2026 in one report. It calls for federal and state laws protecting physicians who choose not to use an AI tool or who decline its recommendation, and places liability for a defective product on the vendor, manufacturer or distributor. Where a hospital, health system or payer mandates a specific tool, the AMA says the mandating entity and the vendor, not the physician, should bear liability (FourWeekMBA and Let's Data Science, both relaying AMA wording as I could see it).
- Axios (13 Nov) reports the AMA raising the case where a tool recommends treatment from incomplete or outdated inputs that the physician cannot verify, and notes existing lawsuits over algorithm-driven decisions, including an Alabama wrongful-death case over a heart screening tool.
- FourWeekMBA cites the AMA 2026 physician survey: 81% of physicians use AI in practice, up from 38% in 2023. I have not checked the survey.

## Context in the Healthcare Brew article

- Many tech companies use a human-in-the-loop model, but doctors can miss errors.
- Hospitals deploying AI tools for staff could expose doctors to malpractice claims. The article says no such cases have been reported, citing Healthcare Dive, but expects one eventually.
- The American College of Physicians' president says AI should help physicians, and that accountability and trust rest on human elements of care that chatbots cannot replicate.
- Courts have, per the article, held AI developers responsible for their tools rather than end users, and legal experts quoted by Modern Healthcare say health systems should monitor tools after deployment.
- Sharif Vakili (physician and UpDoc CEO) compares AI to implantable defibrillators and argues responsibility should track control: developers for safety, health systems for deployment and monitoring, clinicians for their decisions.

## Why a privacy and cyber hub should care

- **Accountability allocation.** The AMA's "responsibility follows control" model is a general template for AI governance: who selects, configures, monitors and can override a system. It matches the monitoring and documentation duties in AI risk programmes.
- **Contract terms.** Indemnities, limitation clauses and consent forms are where liability for AI failures is quietly moved. Vendor due diligence should read them.
- **Human-in-the-loop is not a defence by itself.** The article notes reviewers can miss errors, which bears on how organisations design and evidence oversight.
- **Health data.** Clinical AI processes health data, so incident, transparency and monitoring duties sit alongside privacy law. For another US state AI enforcement development see the [New York ASSET unit page](new-york-ag-asset-ai-enforcement-unit-2026.md).
