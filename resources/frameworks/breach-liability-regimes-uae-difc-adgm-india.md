# Breach liability and security duties — UAE mainland, DIFC, ADGM and India (reference note)

`UAE · India · Breach notification · Penalties · Private right of action · "Reasonable security"`

Prepared for the General Counsel Summit (Dubai, 12 Nov 2026), Session 3. Status as of 12 Nov 2026; check primary texts before relying on any figure.

| Regime | Security / breach duties | Penalties and enforcement | Watch points |
|---|---|---|---|
| **UAE PDPL** (Federal Decree-Law 45/2021), mainland | In force since 2 Jan 2022; supervised by the UAE Data Office (FDL 44/2021) | Detailed breach notification and penalty mechanics depend on Executive Regulations | Law firms report the Executive Regulations have **not been issued** (Al Tamimi; Morgan Lewis, June 2026); some commercial sites claim otherwise, so confirm on the official legislation portal. On 14 June 2026 a Federal Authority for Artificial Intelligence and Data was announced; the market expects it to take over finalising the regulations |
| **DIFC Data Protection Law No. 5 of 2020** (amended July 2025) | Controllers must notify the Commissioner of a breach without delay | Administrative fines in Schedule 2 (USD 10,000–100,000 range per Chambers, with higher-tier fixed fines added in 2025). **Private right of action in the DIFC Courts** (Art. 64A) from the July 2025 amendment, covering material and non-material damage | First tests of damages claims; burden-of-proof wording favours claimants |
| **ADGM Data Protection Regulations 2021** | Report to the Commissioner within **72 hours**; processors must tell controllers immediately | ADGM fined Okadoc Technologies USD 20,000 (21 May 2024) over a data-subject access failure | Smaller fines in practice, but published decisions are guidance |
| **DIFC Regulation 10** (AI / autonomous systems; Data Protection Regulations) | In force 1 Sept 2023; full enforcement from 1 Jan 2026. High-risk processing requires an Autonomous Systems Officer, certification, registers and transparency | Enforced through the DIFC Commissioner | Consultation Paper No. 3 of 2026 (closed 18 July 2026) proposes tightening it and adding a Regulation 11 on accreditation and certification. Relevant to AI-agent deployments |
| **UAE Federal Decree-Law 34/2021 (Cybercrime)** | Criminal offences for unauthorised access and related conduct | Criminal | See the hub's [cybercrime analysis](../../analyses/uae-cybercrime-law-offences.md) |
| **India DPDP Act 2023 and Rules 2025** | Rule 6 security safeguards (encryption, access control, log retention); Rule 7: intimation to the Board and affected individuals without delay, then a detailed report within 72 hours; every breach reportable | Up to ₹250 crore for failure to maintain reasonable security safeguards; up to ₹200 crore for failure to notify | Rules notified 13 Nov 2025; Consent Manager registration from about 13 Nov 2026; core obligations from 13 May 2027; relevant for Indian group entities |
| **EU AI Act Art. 15** (cross-reference) | Robustness, cybersecurity and accuracy for high-risk AI systems | Via the AI Act enforcement regime | Useful when discussing AI-agent security |
| **GDPR Arts. 32–34** (comparator) | Appropriate technical and organisational measures; 72-hour notification | Up to 2%/4% of turnover tiers | Marriott/Starwood and Meta show the enforcement style |

**Notes from a supplementary research note (unverified here):** the PDPL six-month conformity period only starts on issuance of the Executive Regulations; the Cybercrime Law's Articles 6 and 13 address unauthorised access and misuse of personal data. Check the texts before citing.

## Standards to cite for "reasonable security measures"

ISO/IEC 27001/27002 (ISMS and controls), ISO/IEC 27701 (privacy), ISO/IEC 27035 (incident management), NIST CSF 2.0, and for AI systems ISO/IEC 27090 (see the [hub note](iso-27090-ai-security.md)) and the [NIST IR 8596 Cyber AI Profile](nist-ir-8596-cyber-ai-profile.md). None of these is a legal safe harbour in the UAE; use them as the evidence of what a reasonable organisation does.
