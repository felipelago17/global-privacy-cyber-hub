# Case study — SEC v. SolarWinds and its CISO (filed Oct 2023; dismissed with prejudice Nov 2025)

`United States · Securities law · Cyber disclosure · CISO liability · "Reasonable" security statements`

## Summary

In October 2023 the SEC sued SolarWinds and its CISO, Timothy Brown, over statements about the company's cybersecurity practices and its disclosures after the 2020 "Sunburst" attack. It was the first SEC cybersecurity enforcement action against an individual CISO, and the first to base accounting-controls claims on technical security failings. In July 2024 the court dismissed most claims, including those based on the post-attack disclosures, which it said relied on "hindsight and speculation". It kept the claims about the pre-attack security statement on SolarWinds' website. The SEC and the defendants jointly stipulated to dismiss the remaining claims with prejudice on 20 November 2025.

## Key facts

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Court | US District Court, Southern District of New York (Judge Engelmayer) |
| Dismissed July 2024 | Fraud and false-filing claims on post-attack disclosures; internal accounting controls claims; claims based on blog posts, podcasts and press statements as non-actionable puffery |
| Survived July 2024 | Fraud claims on the pre-attack website security statement, against the company and the CISO |
| End | Joint stipulation, 20 Nov 2025; dismissed with prejudice |

## Why it matters

- **What you say publicly about security is a liability surface.** The claims that survived were about a security statement that allegedly did not match internal reality. This is the practical content of "demonstrating transparency and accountability".
- **Specific statements are riskier than general ones:** the court treated general statements as puffery but not a specific, checkable security statement.
- **CISO exposure:** the court accepted that a CISO can be a primary defendant, but the SEC ultimately dropped the case; the signal is mixed and the risk is not gone.
- **For a UAE audience:** no exact equivalent exists, but misleading security statements can arise under consumer, securities and free-zone regimes, and in vendor due-diligence responses.
