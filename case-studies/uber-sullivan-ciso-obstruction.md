# Case study — United States v. Sullivan: the Uber CSO convicted over a breach cover-up (2016 breach; conviction 2022; affirmed 2025)

`United States · Personal liability · Breach concealment · Regulator investigations · Bug bounty`

## Summary

Joseph Sullivan, Uber's Chief Security Officer from 2015 to 2017, was convicted in 2022 of obstruction of justice and misprision of a felony for covering up a 2016 breach that exposed the personal data of 57 million users and about 600,000 driver's licence numbers. The breach was handled as a "bug bounty" payment to the hackers, who signed non-disclosure agreements, while the FTC was investigating Uber's security practices. The Ninth Circuit upheld the conviction on 13 March 2025.

## Key facts

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Charges | Obstruction of the FTC investigation; misprision of a felony |
| Breach | 2016 incident; 57m users and ~600,000 driver's licence numbers |
| Sentence | Three years' probation, 200 hours' community service, US$50,000 fine; prosecutors had sought 15 months in prison |
| Appeal | Ninth Circuit unanimously affirmed on 13 March 2025; treating the intrusion as a bug bounty and using NDAs did not remove the illegality. The panel amended its opinion and denied rehearing en banc on 12 Nov 2025 |
| Further step | A petition for a writ of certiorari (No. 25-1082) was filed at the US Supreme Court in March 2026; outcome not checked |

## Why it matters for a GC / compliance lead

- **Personal exposure:** the first criminal prosecution of a corporate security officer over breach handling. Decisions about who is told, and when, are legal decisions, not only security ones.
- **Regulator-in-the-room risk:** the offence turned on concealment *while a regulator was already investigating*.
- **Bug bounty is not a safe harbour** for stolen personal data; payments and NDAs need legal sign-off.
- **Reporting lines:** Sullivan was also a deputy general counsel; the case is a prompt to document who made each disclosure decision.
